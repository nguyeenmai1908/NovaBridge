The 1:1 with Priya

You'll play as Jordan, a new engineering manager. The choices you make shape how the conversation goes. There are no wrong endings, only different ones.

Jordan (you)
Priya

You are Jordan.

You got promoted to engineering manager eight months ago. Before that, you were the person everyone came to when something broke. Five years of being the best individual contributor on the team.

Nobody questioned the promotion. But nobody taught you how to manage people either.

Priya reports to you.

She's a mid-level developer. Smart, quiet, the kind of person who picks up other people's loose ends without being asked. She was on the team before you became her manager.

Her sprint tickets have been incomplete three cycles in a row. You've noticed. You haven't said anything.

From: Alex Chen (your manager)
"Hey, can we talk about Priya's output? The team lead flagged it again. Just want to make sure you're across it."

Your 1:1 with Priya is in 30 minutes.

You've been telling yourself you'd bring it up when the time felt right. The time never felt right. Now your manager is asking.

Priya Rajan
1:1 · Thursday 2:00pm

Day 3
Your manager follows up: "Did you get a chance to talk to Priya? Team lead flagged two more incomplete tickets this sprint."
You reply: "Yeah, we chatted. Keeping an eye on it."
Day 7
Priya misses a client-facing deliverable. The account manager escalates directly to your manager. You find out in a Slack channel with six other people in it.
Day 10
Your manager calls a meeting. Tone: measured, not hostile. Message: "I need you to have a documented conversation with Priya this week. If this continues, HR will be involved."
The conversation you avoided for two weeks now has to happen under formal pressure.
Day 14
You have the conversation. It is reactive, defensive, and short. Priya didn't know she was being documented. She feels ambushed.
After the meeting she sits at her desk for a while. Then she opens LinkedIn.

Priya had been covering for a colleague on leave for six weeks.
She never told anyone — because no one asked.

Six weeks after this conversation, she accepted an offer at another company.

Jordan never found out what was actually going on.

Private reflection

What do you now understand about why you went to a solution before asking a question?

You asked.

That's the thing most managers in Jordan's position don't do — not because they're bad managers, but because asking feels slower than solving.

You asked anyway. And the conversation became something neither of you expected it to be.

Private reflection

Think about the conversation you came in with. What's one thing you now know about it that you didn't know at the start?

You found the real problem. That's the hardest part — and you got there.

But the way you closed removed something important: Priya's role in the solution.

When a manager solves a problem for someone rather than with them, the person learns to wait for the manager to solve the next one.
