Sarah
Sarah — unspoken

It's 2:47pm on a Tuesday.

Sarah has a 1:1 with Daniel in thirteen minutes.

Daniel is one of the people she managed for eight months before her promotion. They used to get coffee on Thursdays. He was at her promotion dinner.

In the last three weeks, Daniel has missed two check-ins, submitted a client proposal with three factual errors, and replied to Sarah's Slack message with a thumbs-up emoji and nothing else.

Sarah opens her notes app.
She starts typing what she wants to say.

She closes it.

She'll bring it up if it feels right in the meeting.

Observed

The meeting starts. Daniel looks tired.
Sarah asks how the Henderson proposal is coming along.
Daniel says fine.
Sarah asks about his workload.
Daniel says manageable.
Sarah says great, keep her posted.

The meeting ends at 3:14.
Sarah marks it complete in her calendar.

She did not bring it up.

If you recognized something in that — you're in the right place.

Sarah isn't the only one.

Marcus

Marcus was promoted to tech lead six months ago after five years as the strongest engineer on the team.

He noticed that one of his developers, Jin, had been submitting code with increasing errors over the past month. Not critical errors. Just more than before.

Marcus figured Jin would sort it out.
That's what Marcus would have done.

He mentioned it once, loosely, in a group standup:

"Let's all make sure we're reviewing before we push."

Jin nodded along with everyone else.

The errors continued.
Amara

Amara manages a six-person operations team. Her own manager, David, is known for keeping things smooth.

In two years, Amara has never seen David address a performance issue directly. She has seen three people quietly moved to different teams. She has seen one person managed out over the course of nine months without a single direct conversation.

When one of Amara's team members started missing deadlines, Amara's first instinct was to redistribute the work quietly. The same way David would.

It felt like the professional thing to do.
