# STAGE 2 — UNDERSTANDING
## "Here's why it's actually hard — and it's not what you think."
## Estimated time: 10 minutes

---

### SCREEN 2.1 — The three real reasons

[NARRATION]
Most training on difficult conversations skips this part.
It goes straight to: here's a framework, here's what to say.

But if you don't understand why this is hard for you specifically,
the framework won't stick.
It'll feel like something someone invented in a conference room.

So before anything else — three reasons.
You'll probably recognize at least one of them.

---

### SCREEN 2.2 — Reason 1 (Sarah's pattern)

[SCREEN — section marker]
Reason 1 of 3

[SCREEN — heading]
The relationship cost feels more certain than the benefit.

[NARRATION]
Sarah has been friends with Daniel for three years.
She knows his kids' names. She was at his wedding.

When she imagines having the conversation,
she can feel exactly how it changes things.
The warmth in the room drops.
He looks at her differently after.
Maybe he tells the others.

The benefit of the conversation is uncertain —
maybe Daniel improves. Maybe the proposal quality comes back.
Maybe things go back to how they were.

But the cost feels certain.
So she protects the relationship the only way that feels safe:
by saying nothing.

---

[NARRATION]
Here is what Sarah doesn't see:

The relationship is already changing.

Daniel knows something is off.
He doesn't know what — but he senses that Sarah
has been asking softer questions than usual.
That the check-ins feel more like checkboxes.

Silence doesn't preserve a relationship.
It just makes the change invisible
until it's too late to reverse it.

---

[SCREEN — small data point, appears after narration]
In a global study, 85% of employees reported experiencing
workplace conflict — and the average employee spends
2.8 hours per week dealing with it.
That's nearly a full day every month lost to tension
that could be addressed directly.
(Source: CPP Global Human Capital Report)

---

### SCREEN 2.3 — Reason 2 (Marcus's pattern)

[SCREEN — section marker]
Reason 2 of 3

[SCREEN — heading]
The skills that got you promoted are working against you.

[NARRATION]
Marcus spent five years becoming exceptional
by figuring things out himself.

When he didn't know something, he found the answer.
When something was broken, he fixed it.
He never needed someone to hold his hand —
and he respected people who didn't need theirs held either.

So when Jin starts making more errors,
Marcus's instinct is to give Jin space.
Let him work through it.
That's what Marcus would have wanted.

---

[NARRATION]
But Marcus is no longer being measured
on what he figures out himself.

He's being measured on what his team figures out —
with his help.

What made him excellent as an individual contributor
is now working against the people he manages.

Jin doesn't interpret Marcus's silence as respect for his autonomy.
Jin interprets it as: Marcus hasn't noticed.
Or worse: Marcus has noticed and doesn't think it's worth addressing.

---

[NARRATION]
The transition from individual contributor to manager
is one of the most difficult career shifts professionals face —
not because the new role is harder,
but because the skills that built your reputation
can become liabilities in the new one.

This isn't a failure of character.
It's a mismatch between old strengths and new context.

---

### SCREEN 2.4 — Reason 3 (Amara's pattern)

[SCREEN — section marker]
Reason 3 of 3

[SCREEN — heading]
The system has taught you that avoidance is safer.

[NARRATION]
Amara isn't avoiding the conversation because she's afraid
of conflict or because she doesn't know how.

She's avoiding it because her organization has spent
two years teaching her — through example, not policy —
that the person who surfaces a problem
becomes associated with the problem.

She has watched David move people quietly rather than
address performance directly.
She has seen what happens when managers escalate to HR —
more process, more scrutiny, more risk for the manager.

Avoidance, in Amara's context, is not a personal failure.
It is a rational response to a real organizational signal.

---

[NARRATION]
We're not going to pretend that signal doesn't exist.

In some organizations, direct conversations do carry risk.
That is an organizational design problem —
not something one training can fix.

But here's what we can tell you:

The cost of avoidance compounds quietly
until it becomes visible loudly.

For Amara's team member, the missed deadlines are already
starting to affect the rest of the team.
Two people are picking up the slack without being asked.
One of them is starting to wonder why they should bother.

The conversation Amara is avoiding to protect herself
is starting to cost her in exactly the ways
she was trying to avoid.

---

### SCREEN 2.5 — The biology moment

[SCREEN — heading]
One more thing. This one is about your brain.

[NARRATION]
When you imagine having a difficult performance conversation,
your brain treats it the same way it treats physical danger.

The amygdala — the part of your brain responsible for
fear and stress — activates in social conflict
the same way it activates when something physically threatens you.

Your heart rate goes up slightly.
Your thinking becomes less flexible.
Your instinct is to escape the situation.

That is not a character flaw.
That is your brain working exactly as it was designed to work.

---

[NARRATION]
The goal is not to stop feeling nervous.

The goal is to have a tool reliable enough
that you can act even when you do.

---

### SCREEN 2.6 — The real cost

[NARRATION]
One number before we move on.

[SCREEN — stat, large]
$7,500

[NARRATION]
That is the estimated cost — in lost productivity,
management time, and downstream team impact —
of every difficult conversation that is not held.

Not every terminated employee.
Not every resignation.

Every. Avoided. Conversation.

# STAGE 3 — THE TOOL
## "Here's what to say — and why each part works."
## Estimated time: 10 minutes

---

### SCREEN 3.1 — The real question

[NARRATION]
Every manager we've described in this program
knows they should have the conversation.

That's not the problem.

The problem is the question underneath:

[SCREEN — large, centered]
"But what do I actually say?"

[NARRATION]
What follows is not a script.
It's a framework — four parts,
each one designed to solve a specific fear
that makes the conversation hard to start.

You've already met those fears in Stage 2.
Now here's what answers each of them.

---

### SCREEN 3.2 — Part O: Observe

[SCREEN — framework marker]
O · Observe

[SCREEN — the fear it solves]
Fear: "If I bring this up, it'll sound like a personal attack."

[NARRATION]
Most managers who avoid performance conversations
aren't generally conflict-averse.

They're afraid of being perceived as unfair.
As attacking someone's character.
As making it personal when it's supposed to be professional.

The Observe step solves this directly.

---

[SCREEN — contrast, two versions of Marcus]

Marcus without this step:
"Your work has been sloppy lately."

Marcus with this step:
"I noticed the last three code reviews had errors
that weren't caught before push — on March 3rd, 10th, and 18th."

---

[NARRATION]
The difference is not tone.
It's not even empathy.
It's specificity.

A vague observation sounds like a judgment.
A specific observation sounds like a fact —
because it is one.

The test: could a camera have recorded what you're describing?

If yes — you're observing.
If no — you're judging.

Only one of those opens a conversation.
The other one closes it.

---

[NARRATION]
Before any performance conversation,
complete this sentence:

"I noticed _______ on _______ [specific date or context]."

If you can't complete it, you're not ready for the conversation yet.
Gather more information first.

---

### SCREEN 3.3 — Part N: Name the impact

[SCREEN — framework marker]
N · Name the impact

[SCREEN — the fear it solves]
Fear: "This will make it about my feelings, not about the work."

[NARRATION]
Managers trying to be empathetic often soften
performance conversations with personal language:

"It's been frustrating for the team."
"I've been worried about you."
"People have been noticing."

The intention is kind.
The effect is the opposite of kind.

Personal language forces the employee to respond
to someone's feelings — which is uncomfortable,
hard to engage with, and easy to get defensive about.

---

[SCREEN — contrast, two versions of Amara]

Amara without this step:
"It's been frustrating when deadlines get missed.
The team has been feeling the pressure."

Amara with this step:
"When the Thursday reports come in late,
the Monday client briefing goes out without complete data.
That's created two client escalations in the past month."

---

[NARRATION]
A feeling is something to manage.
A business consequence is something to solve.

Name the consequence — not the feeling —
and the conversation shifts from personal to professional.

That shift is what makes it possible
for the other person to actually engage with you,
rather than defend themselves.

---

[NARRATION]
Before any performance conversation,
complete this sentence:

"When this happens, it means _______."

If the answer is a feeling — keep going.
Find the consequence underneath the feeling.
That's what belongs in the conversation.

---

### SCREEN 3.4 — Part A: Ask first

[SCREEN — framework marker]
A · Ask first

[SCREEN — the fear it solves]
Fear: "I already know what's wrong — asking wastes time."

[NARRATION]
This is the most important step.

It is also the step that feels the most unnecessary
to managers like Marcus — who have spent years
getting good at diagnosing problems and solving them fast.

Here's the question we want you to sit with for a moment:

[SCREEN — large, centered]
What percentage of the time do you think managers
correctly identify the root cause of a performance issue
— before asking the employee about it?

[UI — no input needed, just a pause]

[NARRATION — appears after 4 seconds]
Research suggests: less than half the time.

Not because managers are bad at diagnosis.
Because performance issues at work almost always have
a context the manager can't see from the outside.

---

[SCREEN — the moment that changes everything]

[NARRATION]
Here is what happens when Marcus assumes versus when he asks.

---

Marcus assumes:
He tells Jin to be more careful before pushing code.
Jin says he will.
The errors continue.
Marcus escalates.
HR gets involved.
Jin resigns four months later.
In his exit interview, Jin mentions that he'd been covering
for a colleague on medical leave for six weeks —
and no one had redistributed the work.

The errors were not a skill problem.
They were a capacity problem.
One question would have changed everything.

---

Marcus asks:
"Is there something getting in the way
that I'm not aware of?"

Jin pauses.

Then: "Actually — yeah. Since Kenji went on leave,
I've been pulling double reviews. I didn't want to complain."

Marcus adjusts the workload that afternoon.
The errors stop the following week.

---

[NARRATION]
The ask is not a formality.
It is not a warm-up before you give your diagnosis.

It is the moment where you find out
whether you're solving the right problem.

And when the answer surprises you —
which it will, more often than you expect —
the conversation stops being uncomfortable
and starts being useful.

---

[NARRATION]
Before any performance conversation,
write your question:

"Is there something _______ that I'm not aware of?"

Do not skip this step.
Whatever you think the answer is —
ask anyway.

---

### SCREEN 3.5 — Part A: Agree on next steps

[SCREEN — framework marker]
A · Agree on next steps

[SCREEN — the fear it solves]
Fear: "Even if the conversation goes well, nothing will change."

[NARRATION]
This fear is earned.

Many managers have had performance conversations
that felt productive in the room
and produced no change outside it.

The reason is almost always the same:
the conversation ended without a specific agreement.

---

[SCREEN — contrast, two versions of Sarah]

Sarah without this step:
"I'm glad we talked about this, Daniel.
Let's make sure we keep communicating.
I'm always here if you need me."

Daniel leaves the meeting unclear on what's expected.
Sarah leaves the meeting feeling like she addressed it.
Nothing changes.

---

Sarah with this step:
"Here's what I'd like to do.
I'll check in with the Henderson team today
about the proposal timeline.
Can you send me a revised version by Thursday
with the three sections we talked about?
We'll look at it together Friday morning."

Daniel has a specific action.
Sarah has a specific touchpoint.
Both of them know what happens next.

---

[NARRATION]
The difference between a performance conversation
that changes behavior
and one that doesn't
almost always lives in the last two minutes.

Vague agreements feel complete.
Specific agreements create accountability —
without making either person feel cornered.

---

[NARRATION]
Before any performance conversation,
draft your close:

"I'll _______ by _______. Can you _______ by _______?"

Named action. Named person. Named date.
Both sides have something to do.
Both sides know when.

---

### SCREEN 3.6 — The full framework

[SCREEN — ONAA card, assembled]

O · Observe
Describe the specific behavior. Facts only.
"I noticed _______ on _______."

N · Name the impact
Connect the behavior to a business consequence.
"When this happens, it means _______."

A · Ask first
Ask an open question before proposing anything.
"Is there something _______ I'm not aware of?"

A · Agree on next steps
Co-create a specific, time-bound action.
"I'll _______ by _______. Can you _______ by _______?"

---

[NARRATION]
This card is in your job aid — available any time from the nav.
Use it to prepare before any performance conversation.
You don't need to memorize it.
You just need to have it open.

---

# JOB AID — Pre-conversation checklist
## Always available · not gated · downloadable PDF

---

### JOB AID PAGE

[HEADER]
Before you go in.

[SUBHEADER]
Five questions to answer before any performance conversation.
You don't need to memorize this.
Open it, answer it, go in.

---

[CARD 1 — O]
1 · What specific behavior did I observe?

Not a feeling. Not a judgment.
Something a camera could have recorded.

"I noticed _______ on _______."

If you can't complete this sentence —
you're not ready yet. Gather more information first.

---

[CARD 2 — N]
2 · What did that behavior cost?

Not how it made you feel.
What did it cost the team, the client, the business?

"When this happens, it means _______."

If your answer is a feeling — keep going.
Find the consequence underneath.

---

[CARD 3 — A]
3 · What question haven't I asked yet?

Before you walk in with a solution —
write the question you'll ask first.

"Is there something _______ I'm not aware of?"

Do not skip this.
Whatever you think the answer is — ask anyway.

---

[CARD 4 — A]
4 · What will we each do next?

Draft your close before you go in.

"I'll _______ by _______.
Can you _______ by _______?"

Named person. Named action. Named date.
Both sides. Both accountable.

---

[CARD 5 — Meta question]
5 · What am I assuming that might be wrong?

This question isn't in any framework.
It's the one that makes all four steps above work.

Write one assumption you're bringing in.
Be willing to discover you're wrong about it.
