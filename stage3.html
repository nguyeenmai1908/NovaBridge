O
Observe

Most managers open performance conversations with a feeling or a label. "Your attitude." "Your effort." "Your work." The Observe step replaces that with something specific enough that a camera could have recorded it. Not a judgment. Not an interpretation. A fact.

The reason most managers skip this, or get it wrong, comes down to one fear:

⚠️ "If I bring this up, it'll sound like a personal attack."

Here's what that fear looks like in practice, and what changes when you use this step.

✗ Without this step
"Your work has been sloppy lately."
Vague. Sounds like a personal attack. Marcus can't engage with it.
→
✓ With Observe
"I noticed the last three code reviews had errors that weren't caught before push — on March 3rd, 10th, and 18th."
Specific. Factual. Hard to argue with.

The test: could a camera have recorded what you're describing?

If yes — you're observing. If no — you're judging.

Question 1 of 4

N
Name the impact

After you describe the behavior, you need to connect it to something real. Not how it made you feel. What it actually cost the team or the business. That shift changes the whole tone of the conversation.

The fear that stops most managers here:

⚠️ "This will make it about my feelings, not about the work."

Here's the difference between naming a feeling and naming a consequence.

✗ Without this step
"It's been frustrating when deadlines get missed. The team has been feeling the pressure."
Feelings. The employee has to manage your emotion, not solve the problem.
→
✓ With Name the impact
"When the Thursday reports come in late, the Monday client briefing goes out without complete data. That's created two escalations this month."
Business consequence. Something to solve together.

A feeling is something to manage. A consequence is something to solve together.

A
Ask first

Before you propose any solution, you ask one open question. This is the step most managers skip, and the one that changes everything. Because you almost never have the full picture from the outside.

The instinct that gets in the way:

⚠️ "I already know what's wrong. Asking wastes time."

Here's what happens when Marcus assumes versus when he asks.

✗ Without this step
"I think you need to be more disciplined about your sprint planning."
Assumes the cause. Might be solving the wrong problem entirely.
→
✓ With Ask first
"Is there something getting in the way that I'm not aware of?"
Opens the door. The real answer is almost never what you expect.

A
Agree on next steps

The conversation isn't done when it feels good. It's done when both sides know exactly what happens next. Named action, named person, named date. Both sides.

The reason most conversations end without real change:

⚠️ "Even if the conversation goes well, nothing will change."

Here's the difference between closing warmly and closing specifically.

✗ Without this step
"Let's make sure we keep communicating. My door is always open."
Feels complete. Changes nothing.
→
✓ With Agree next steps
"I'll talk to the team lead today. Can you send me a list of what you've been carrying by end of day Friday?"
Named actions. Named date. Both sides accountable.

Close with something specific:

"I'll   by  . Can you   by  ?"
